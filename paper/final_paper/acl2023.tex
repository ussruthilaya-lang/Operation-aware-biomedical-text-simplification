\begin{abstract}
Biomedical text simplification systems typically apply a single, undifferentiated
simplification strategy to all text, regardless of what kind of complexity a given
span actually exhibits. We argue that classifying the \emph{operation} a span
requires -- Substitution, Explanation, or Generalization -- before generation
produces safer and more capable simplification than unguided end-to-end approaches.
We introduce a multi-signal complexity detection framework spanning lexical,
syntactic, numerical, and safety-critical dimensions, grounded in a validated
real-UMLS terminology lookup achieving complete jargon coverage on our training set
(100.0\%, vs.\ 86.8\% for a morphological heuristic baseline). Our operation
classifier progresses from a TF-IDF baseline (0.424 macro-F1) through a
general-domain contextual model (DistilBERT, 0.392, a \emph{regression}) to a
biomedical-domain contextual model (BioBERT, 0.465, $+9.7\%$ over TF-IDF),
demonstrating that domain-specific pretraining, not contextual pretraining alone,
is what predicts operation type. A controlled feature-integration ablation on the
TF-IDF classifier shows that explicit domain features (UMLS jargon density,
numerical density) improve macro-F1 by a further $2.1$--$2.5\%$ when correctly
isolated from a confounding vocabulary-size change in an earlier draft of this
experiment; whether the same features improve BioBERT specifically remains
future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). Most
importantly, we evaluate the complete operation-aware pipeline -- classification,
CHV-based substitution, and constrained generation -- against three baselines on
the held-out PLABA test set. Our system achieves a SARI of 21.72, exceeding
rule-based substitution (21.12) while matching its perfect warning preservation
(1.000), and narrows the SARI gap to an unguided LLM baseline (25.49, but only
0.980 warning preservation and 0.925 numerical preservation) to 3.77 points
without any of its safety cost. On a warning-stratified stress test -- the
subset of sentences where safety failures actually concentrate -- our system's
SARI (25.43) \emph{exceeds} the unguided LLM baseline's (24.73) while still
holding perfect warning preservation where the baseline drops to 0.980: on
precisely the sentences where safety matters most, our system is simultaneously
safer and more readable, not merely safer at a readability cost. We further show
that complexity detectors can serve not only as classifier features but as an
explicit, verified guardrail during generation itself: a CHV-then-polish step
that injects detected numerical values and warning phrases as protected spans
into the generation prompt, and rejects any output that fails to preserve them,
recovers readability that plain dictionary substitution cannot achieve while
fully retaining safety. Finally, we show that safety evaluation for
simplification systems requires warning-stratified sampling, not random sampling,
to reliably detect rare but safety-critical failures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Patient-facing biomedical text -- research abstracts, clinical notes, drug
labeling -- is frequently written at a reading and vocabulary level that assumes
clinical training the intended reader does not have. Low health literacy is linked
to worse health outcomes \citep{berkman2011health}, so making biomedical text
readable is a practical clinical concern, not only a linguistic one. Automated
simplification systems aim to close this gap, but most treat simplification as a
single, undifferentiated transformation applied uniformly across a text: a
sentence is fed into a model and a simpler sentence is returned, whether the
difficulty came from a single jargon term, a tangled clause structure, or a
critical dosage.

We argue this is the wrong framing. PLABA's own five-operation taxonomy
\citep{attal2023plaba} shows that ``simplify'' is not one transformation but
several distinct ones -- Substitution, Explanation, Generalization, Omission, and
Exemplification -- and prior work at the TSAR 2025 shared task confirms that no
submitted system, across 48 submissions from 20 teams, classifies which operation
a span needs before generating output \citep{alvamanchego2025tsarfindings}. We take
a diagnostic approach instead: classify first, then simplify. Before any text is
rewritten, we identify what type of complexity a span carries, and only then apply
the operation suited to it. A jargon term can be replaced from a controlled
vocabulary with no model involved and zero hallucination risk. An unfamiliar
concept can be explained. An overly specific statement can be generalized. Routing
each span to the right operation lets us simplify aggressively where it is safe
and stay conservative where information must be preserved.

Our contributions are: (1) a multi-signal complexity detection framework
combining five orthogonal detectors (near-zero pairwise correlation, strongest
$\phi = 0.078$) with a validated, production-grade UMLS jargon matcher achieving
complete coverage on our training set; (2) an operation classifier progression
(TF-IDF $\rightarrow$ DistilBERT $\rightarrow$ BioBERT) isolating the effect of
biomedical-domain pretraining specifically, plus a controlled ablation showing
which of our detected features genuinely improve classification versus which are
better reserved for safety constraints at generation time; (3) the first
end-to-end evaluation of an operation-aware simplification pipeline against
uniform baselines, showing it improves readability over safe-but-limited
rule-based substitution while retaining that baseline's safety profile; (4) a
mechanism for using complexity detectors as \emph{generation-time guardrails}, not
only classifier inputs, verified post-hoc rather than merely requested of the
generator; and (5) a methodological finding that safety evaluation for
simplification systems requires warning-stratified sampling, since random sampling
is statistically underpowered to detect rare but safety-critical failures.

\section{The Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

The case for operation-awareness is not merely conceptual; it is visible directly
in how differently PLABA's own annotators edited text. Ondov et al.'s reflection
on the TREC PLABA track reports that, across the real human-annotated corpus,
Substitution accounts for $64.7\%$ of edits, Explanation for $19.3\%$,
Generalization for $6.3\%$, with the remainder split across Omission and
Exemplification \citep{ondov2025lessons}. These operations are not interchangeable:
a system that only performs Substitution -- as a rule-based lexical lookup
necessarily does -- is structurally incapable of producing the $19.3\%$ of edits
that require adding an explanation, or the smaller but clinically significant
fraction that require removing specificity while preserving meaning. Conversely, a
system that treats every edit as free-form generation, as an unguided large
language model does, can perform any operation but has no mechanism preventing it
from also silently degrading content that must not change.

The stakes of getting this wrong are not symmetric. Consider a source sentence
\emph{``Do not exceed 2.5\,mg daily''} simplified to \emph{``Take the medicine
daily.''} The simplified sentence is shorter and more readable by every standard
metric, and would score reasonably under SARI or FKGL -- but it has silently
dropped both the maximum-dose warning and the exact dosage, converting a
safety-critical instruction into a vague, dangerous one. A dropped adjective is a
stylistic loss; a dropped contraindication or a garbled dosage is a patient-safety
failure. Standard simplification metrics (SARI, FKGL, BERTScore) cannot
distinguish between these two kinds of edits, since neither is designed to check
\emph{what} content survived, only how well the output matches a reference
distributionally \citep{alvamanchego2021unsuitability}. This motivates both our
operation-aware architecture, which routes safety-sensitive spans away from
free-form generation by construction, and our custom preservation metrics
(Section~\ref{sec:safety-eval}), which make this distinction measurable rather
than anecdotal.

\section{Main Idea}
\label{sec:main-idea}

Figure-free summary of the architecture: a source sentence is first passed through
five complexity detectors (Section~\ref{sec:complexity-detection}) for corpus-level
diagnosis and safety-constraint extraction, and separately through an operation
classifier that predicts one of Substitution, Explanation, or Generalization
(Section~\ref{sec:classifier-results}). The predicted operation determines the
generation strategy: Substitution routes to a Consumer Health Vocabulary (CHV)
lookup, a deterministic, auditable dictionary substitution with zero hallucination
risk; Explanation and Generalization route to a constrained large language model
(FLAN-T5) prompted with an operation-specific instruction, rather than a single
generic ``simplify this'' prompt.

Two design decisions in this architecture were validated empirically rather than
assumed. First, which complexity-detector outputs should feed the classifier as
features: our ablation (Section~\ref{sec:feature-ablation}) shows that UMLS
jargon-density features carry real, coefficient-verified classification signal,
while warning-cue features do not -- and we do \emph{not} treat this as a failure
of the warning detector, but as evidence that its role belongs downstream, as a
generation-time constraint, not a classification signal. This is precisely how we
use it: rather than only informing which operation a span receives, the numerical
and warning detectors are reused a second time, after generation, as an explicit
list of protected spans that a post-substitution fluency pass must not alter, with
violations rejected rather than merely discouraged (Section~\ref{sec:chv-polish}).
This is the sense in which complexity detection functions as a
\emph{generation-time guardrail}, not only a diagnostic front-end feeding a
classifier -- a distinction we found necessary to state explicitly, since it is
easy to under-sell a detector's contribution by measuring only its effect on
classifier accuracy.

Second, whether the classifier should use pseudo-labels uncritically: because PLABA
does not ship span-level operation labels, we derive them via a length-ratio
heuristic from aligned source-target pairs (Section~\ref{sec:classifier-results}).
We found this heuristic's label distribution diverges substantially from the real,
human-annotated distribution reported by \citet{ondov2025lessons}, particularly for
Generalization, and we treat the resulting classifier ceiling as a joint function
of task difficulty and label quality rather than presenting it as a hard ceiling on
what any classifier could achieve.

\subsection{Complexity Detection Framework}
\label{sec:complexity-detection}

Before a biomedical sentence can be simplified, the system must first determine
\emph{what kind} of complexity it exhibits. We argue that biomedical text
complexity is not a single dimension but a composite of at least five distinct
signals, each requiring a different detection paradigm:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Numerical expressions} (dosages, risk ratios, $p$-values,
    confidence intervals), detected via regular expressions. These patterns are
    finite and rule-governed, so no learned model is needed to achieve full
    recall on known formats.
    \item \textbf{Safety-critical warning language} (contraindications, adverse
    effects, risk alerts), detected via a hand-built lexicon, negation-aware (a
    sentence stating a warning does \emph{not} apply is not flagged). Because
    these phrases are clinically standardized, a lexicon gives full auditability
    and zero hallucination risk compared to a learned classifier.
    \item \textbf{Named biomedical entities} (diseases, chemicals), detected via
    a supervised NER model (SciSpaCy, BC5CDR-trained; \citealp{neumann2019scispacy}).
    General-domain NER models miss domain-specific entities such as
    \emph{nephrotoxicity}; biomedical pretraining is not optional for this
    signal.
    \item \textbf{Structural complexity}, detected via dependency parse depth
    rather than raw sentence length. Length alone unfairly penalizes
    long-but-simple sentences (e.g., coordinated lists of facts), whereas parse
    depth captures genuine syntactic nesting that a vocabulary-only view misses
    entirely.
    \item \textbf{Medical jargon}, detected via UMLS concept matching
    (Section~\ref{sec:quickumls}). A term is treated as jargon if it exists in
    UMLS as a formal concept but is not part of common English vocabulary.
\end{itemize}

No single paradigm captures all five signals: regular expressions catch numbers
but miss entities; a lexicon catches warnings but misses syntactic structure; NER
catches entities but misses jargon that is not a named entity in the BC5CDR sense
(e.g., procedure names, qualifiers). We treat this as the central architectural
argument motivating a multi-detector design rather than a single end-to-end
classifier -- an argument reinforced empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:complexity-results}, where pairwise correlation between all five
detectors is near zero.

\subsection{QuickUMLS Integration}
\label{sec:quickumls}

Our jargon detector is built on the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS), a
meta-thesaurus unifying over 200 source vocabularies (SNOMED CT, MeSH, RxNorm,
and others) into a single concept space of several million entries. We use
QuickUMLS \citep{Soldaini2016}, which maps free text to UMLS concept identifiers
via approximate string matching, to perform this lookup at the sentence level.

\paragraph{Definition of jargon.} A term is flagged as jargon if it resolves to a
UMLS concept \emph{and} is not part of common English vocabulary, operationalized
via three filters layered on top of raw QuickUMLS output:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Semantic type restriction.} UMLS concepts are tagged with one
    or more of 127+ semantic types. We restrict matches to eight clinically
    relevant types (e.g., \textit{Disease or Syndrome}, \textit{Pharmacologic
    Substance}, \textit{Therapeutic or Preventive Procedure}), excluding generic
    administrative or qualifier concepts that are technically present in UMLS
    but are already plain English (e.g., \textit{primary}, \textit{performed}).
    \item \textbf{Similarity threshold.} We require a QuickUMLS similarity score
    of at least 0.85, filtering out loose or uncertain string matches.
    \item \textbf{Lexical frequency filter.} Semantic-type restriction alone is
    insufficient: some common English words coincidentally match real,
    clinically-relevant UMLS concepts. For example, \emph{control} matches a
    genuine pharmacologic-substance concept at similarity 1.0 -- the same
    semantic type needed to correctly catch real drug names such as
    \emph{sorafenib}. We therefore apply a word-frequency filter using Zipf
    frequency scores \citep{wordfreq}, requiring single-word matches to fall
    below a threshold of 3.8 to be treated as jargon. This threshold was
    validated against a held-out set of 16 known common and jargon terms,
    cleanly separating common words (\emph{control}: 5.40, \emph{determine}:
    4.63, \emph{disease}: 4.90) from genuine jargon (\emph{carcinoma}: 3.15,
    \emph{hyperglycemia}: 2.31, \emph{sorafenib}: 1.43). A preliminary attempt
    using a broad English wordlist (NLTK's \texttt{words} corpus) was rejected:
    it classified genuine jargon such as \emph{hyperglycemia} as ordinary
    English, since raw dictionary membership does not capture actual usage
    frequency.
\end{enumerate}

Additionally, since QuickUMLS returns every candidate concept considered for a
matched span rather than a single best match, we deduplicate by retaining only
the highest-similarity candidate per span.

\section{Details, Evaluation, and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Safety Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:safety-eval}

We measure whether a simplification system preserves safety-critical content
(contraindications, adverse effect warnings) using a warning-preservation metric
with paraphrase matching, rather than surface $n$-gram overlap metrics such as
ROUGE. ROUGE cannot distinguish between a system that preserves a warning and one
that silently drops it, provided the rest of the sentence is scored favorably;
our metric targets exactly this failure mode. We similarly measure numerical
preservation (exact dosage and statistic survival) and, where a NER environment is
available, entity preservation.

A central methodological finding from our evaluation is that \textbf{random
sampling is statistically underpowered to detect rare safety failures}.
Warning-bearing sentences occur at a base rate of approximately $5.7\%$ at the
sentence level in our data (separately, $31.3\%$ of abstracts contain at least
one warning-bearing sentence -- these are different statistics over different
units of analysis, and we report both explicitly to avoid an apparent
contradiction between abstract-level and sentence-level rates); a random
sample of 50 sentences showed all baseline
systems achieving perfect warning preservation, a misleadingly optimistic result.
Only a \emph{stratified} stress test -- restricted to warning-bearing sentences
specifically -- revealed that unguided LLM-based simplification is the only
baseline that drops safety-critical content under evaluation. We treat
stratified, targeted evaluation as a required methodological component for
safety-critical NLP systems generally, not specific to this dataset.

The real UMLS pipeline achieves complete (100.0\%) jargon coverage across the

and true terminology-base lookup. We validated this result in two stages: first

cardioverter-defibrillator}, \emph{hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism},

Consistent with our preliminary findings, the majority of abstracts exhibit
multiple simultaneous complexity signals: 98.1\% of abstracts trigger three or
more of our five detectors under the full UMLS pipeline, reinforcing that
biomedical text complexity is multi-dimensional and that a single-signal
detector would systematically under-detect the true complexity profile of this
domain. Pairwise phi-coefficient correlation among the five detectors is close to
zero across the board (strongest: $\phi = 0.078$), indicating the detectors fire
largely independently rather than redundantly -- direct support for the
five-detector design over a single consolidated signal.

\subsection{Operation Classification}
\label{sec:classifier-results}

Predicting the operation a span requires is the research centerpiece. Crucially,
the classifier sees only the \emph{source} sentence: at inference time no target
exists, so any feature derived from the reference would be leakage.

\paragraph{Pseudo-labeling.} Because PLABA lacks public span-level operation
labels, we derive them by weak supervision from aligned source-target pairs,
sentence-aligning \texttt{data.json} and excluding all validation and test PMIDs
to prevent leakage. Each pair is labeled by a length-ratio rule with an
edit-distance tiebreak: a target at least $15\%$ longer is an Explanation, one at
least $15\%$ shorter is a Generalization, and a similar-length pair with
sufficient character-level change is a Substitution. This yields $6{,}307$ labeled
training pairs: $43.2\%$ Substitution, $34.4\%$ Explanation, $22.5\%$
Generalization.

This pseudo-label distribution diverges substantially from the real,
human-annotated distribution that \citet{ondov2025lessons} report for the same
three operations ($64.7\%$ Substitution, $19.3\%$ Explanation, $6.3\%$
Generalization) -- Generalization is roughly $3.6\times$ over-represented in our
pseudo-labels. We believe this reflects a structural limitation of the
length-ratio heuristic: a source sentence that loses a numerically dense
subordinate clause (arguably an Omission-type edit) also shrinks in length and can
be indistinguishable, under a pure length-ratio rule, from a genuinely generalized
statement. We report our classifier results below against this caveat rather than
treating the pseudo-labels as gold.

\paragraph{Classifier progression.} Table~\ref{tab:classifier-progression}
reports macro-F1 across three classifier backbones, isolating the value of
domain-specific versus general contextual pretraining. TF-IDF with
$(1,2)$-gram features and class-weighted logistic regression establishes the
lexical baseline. DistilBERT, a general-domain contextual model, actually
\emph{underperforms} the lexical baseline by $7.5\%$ relative -- general
contextual pretraining without domain adaptation is not sufficient for this task.
BioBERT, pretrained on biomedical text specifically, improves over TF-IDF by
$9.7\%$ relative, and this result reproduced independently within $1\%$ absolute
macro-F1 across three separate training runs (0.465, 0.461, 0.467), indicating the
result is not an artifact of training randomness.

partly to the pseudo-label noise described above, rather than purely to
architectural capacity, since a classifier cannot exceed the reliability of
the labels it is trained against.

\paragraph{Feature integration (corrected ablation).}
\label{sec:feature-ablation}
We tested whether adding our detected UMLS jargon, warning, and numerical
features to the TF-IDF classifier improves operation prediction. An initial
version of this experiment compared a feature-augmented model built with a
smaller TF-IDF vocabulary ($5{,}000$ features) against a baseline built with a
larger one ($50{,}000$ features), confounding the effect of adding features with
the effect of shrinking the vocabulary -- the reported result (a small
\emph{decrease} in macro-F1) was consequently uninterpretable. Re-run with
matched vocabulary size, the corrected result reverses: adding UMLS and
numerical features improves macro-F1 by $2.1$--$2.5\%$ over TF-IDF alone
(Table~\ref{tab:feature-ablation}).

A per-feature-group ablation isolates which features are responsible: UMLS
jargon features alone account for $76\%$ of the total gain, confirmed by
inspecting the trained model's coefficients directly (mean $|$coefficient$|$
for the engineered features is $0.124$, versus $0.084$ for an average TF-IDF
token -- these features are being weighted meaningfully, not diluted by 50,000
lexical dimensions). Warning features contribute approximately zero in
isolation ($+0.0008$ macro-F1), which we do \emph{not} treat as a shortcoming:
warning language indicates whether output is \emph{safe}, not \emph{which
operation} a sentence requires, and its value lies in the generation-time
constraint described in Section~\ref{sec:chv-polish}, not in routing accuracy.
Numerical features are the subtle case: alone, they measurably \emph{hurt}
Generalization F1 ($0.410 \rightarrow 0.387$) despite raw numerical-expression
presence correlating $2.2\times$ higher in true Generalization sentences than
Explanation sentences in the underlying data -- we interpret this as the
numerical signal being entangled with the same pseudo-label noise affecting
Generalization generally, rather than the feature itself being uninformative.

\subsection{Operation-Aware Generation and CHV-then-Polish}
\label{sec:chv-polish}

The predicted operation determines the generation strategy: Substitution routes
to CHV lookup, a deterministic dictionary substitution; Explanation and
Generalization route to FLAN-T5 with an operation-specific constrained prompt
in place of a single generic instruction.

An initial version of the full pipeline, evaluated against the three baselines
on the complete validation set, scored a SARI of only $18.76$ -- barely above
Rule-based CHV's $19.06$ -- because $43\%$ of sentences (Substitution-routed)
received only a flat dictionary word-swap with no fluency improvement. We
addressed this with a second generation step for Substitution-routed sentences:
after CHV substitution, an optional FLAN-T5 fluency-polish pass rewrites the
sentence for naturalness, constrained by an explicit list of protected spans
drawn directly from our complexity detectors -- the numerical expressions and
the CHV replacement terms just inserted -- injected into the prompt as spans
that must not change, and \emph{verified post-hoc}: if any protected span is
absent from the polished output, the pipeline discards it and falls back to the
guaranteed-safe CHV-only result rather than trusting the instruction was
followed. This is the sense in which complexity detection functions as a
generation-time guardrail (Section~\ref{sec:main-idea}), not only a classifier
feature.

An intermediate version of this mechanism, protecting only numerical spans and
CHV replacement terms, raised SARI to $20.09$ but measurably reduced warning
preservation from $1.000$ to $0.986$: a Substitution-routed sentence that
happened to also contain a warning phrase had that phrase left unprotected, and
the polish pass occasionally reworded it. Adding the warning detector's matched
phrases to the same protected-span list fully recovered warning preservation to
$1.000$ \emph{and} pushed SARI higher still, to $20.22$ -- a genuine improvement
on both axes from the same fix, not a trade-off, and itself a small
demonstration of the paper's broader argument: the failure mode only became
visible, and only became fixable, because we evaluated on both a readability
metric and a safety metric simultaneously.

\subsection{Full-System Evaluation}
\label{sec:full-eval}

We report the complete comparison on the held-out PLABA \emph{test} set
($n=1{,}000$ sentences overall; $n=49$ warning-bearing sentences in the
stratified stress test), with the full pipeline configuration -- classifier
hyperparameters, CHV dictionary, and generation prompts -- frozen exactly as
selected during validation, so no test-set information influenced any design
decision. The classifier checkpoint deployed for this evaluation is one of
the three independent BioBERT training runs reported in
Section~\ref{sec:classifier-results} (macro-F1 $0.467$), re-trained under
identical settings after an unrelated infrastructure incident rather than
reused verbatim from the validation-time run; we note this explicitly rather
than implying a single frozen weight file, and its macro-F1's consistency
with the other two runs is itself evidence the specific training instance
does not materially affect the results reported below. All LLM-based
systems use matched decoding (beam search, 4 beams),
so differences in generation strategy do not confound the comparison. The
same evaluation was also run on the validation set during development
($n=1{,}141$ overall / $n=73$ stratified); we report test-set numbers as the
paper's headline result and note the validation-set pattern was consistent
throughout Section~\ref{sec:results}, not contradicted by the held-out run.

On the overall split (Table~\ref{tab:full-results}), our operation-aware
system exceeds rule-based CHV substitution's readability (SARI $21.72$ vs.\
$21.12$) while matching its
perfect warning preservation, and narrows the SARI gap to the unguided LLM
baseline from $4.38$ (rule-based CHV vs.\ Direct LLM) to $3.77$ points without
incurring that baseline's safety cost -- it also exceeds Direct LLM on
numerical preservation ($0.963$ vs.\ $0.925$).

The stratified stress test (Table~\ref{tab:full-results-stratified}) yields a
stronger result than we anticipated: on precisely the sentences where safety
failures concentrate, our system's SARI ($25.43$) \emph{exceeds} Direct LLM's
($24.73$) while retaining perfect warning preservation where Direct LLM drops
to $0.980$. We had expected our system to trade some readability for safety
relative to Direct LLM; on this split it does not trade at all -- it is
simultaneously safer and more readable. We report this without over-claiming
generality: $n=49$ is small, and we treat this specific magnitude as
indicative rather than precise, consistent with our own argument in
Section~\ref{sec:safety-eval} about the statistical power of small
stratified samples. The direction of the result -- operation-aware
dominating Direct LLM on both axes for warning-bearing sentences
specifically -- is nonetheless the paper's strongest single finding, and it
replicates the same qualitative pattern observed on the validation split
during development, rather than appearing only in the held-out evaluation.

We state the overall-split claim precisely: our system does not
Pareto-dominate every baseline on every metric across both splits -- Direct
LLM leads on overall-split SARI -- but it occupies a strictly more favorable
point on the readability-safety trade-off than either simpler alternative,
which is the paper's central empirical claim, and on the safety-critical
stratified subset it dominates outright.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Datasets and prior PLABA systems.}
PLABA \citep{attal2023plaba} pairs biomedical abstracts with expert
plain-language rewrites at the sentence level, letting us pseudo-label
operations rather than treat simplification as opaque sequence-to-sequence
generation. \citet{knappich2023boschai}'s PLABA-2023-winning BoschAI
system finds that fine-tuned models copy the input and edit
little -- directly motivating our diagnostic framing, since a model that
barely edits also barely distinguishes a safe substitution from a
critical warning. \citet{li2024beemanc} investigate LLMs with
controllable attributes for the same track, including the BART-based and
Lay-SciFive systems we plan as neural baselines in the full paper.

\paragraph{TSAR 2025 and the evaluation gap.}
The winning TSAR 2025 submission \citep{miyata2025ehimenlp} generates
candidates via 28 model-prompt combinations and reranks by semantic
similarity; their own ablation shows the gain comes from diversity and
selection, not from understanding \emph{what kind} of edit a span needs.
The shared task's own findings \citep{alvamanchego2025tsarfindings} show
that across 48 submissions from 20 teams, none classified complexity
type before generating output, and the official metrics (CEFR compliance,
MeaningBERT similarity) cannot detect a fluent output that quietly drops
a warning or dosage -- the organizers explicitly call for
expert-in-the-loop validation as future work. Our preservation metrics
answer this call by making safety a first-class, automatically measurable
criterion rather than deferring it to expert review.

\paragraph{Jargon detection and style robustness.}
\citet{papandreou2025jargon} show jargon detectors trained on MedReadMe
transfer poorly to PLABA ($33.71\%$ entity F1), since the datasets define
jargon under different annotation objectives -- supporting our decision to
build detectors natively on PLABA. Their jargon-aware LLM prompts also
introduce factual errors a lookup table structurally cannot produce,
motivating our CHV-based Substitution pathway over jargon-surfacing
prompts. \citet{rahman2025spqa} find that stylistic perturbations
significantly degrade LLM QA correctness and completeness even when
fluency stays stable, external evidence that surface-level metrics can
miss real content degradation -- the same gap our safety metrics target.

\paragraph{Prior operation-aware simplification.}
\citet{cripwell2022operation} propose controllable simplification via
operation classification as a predict-then-execute process, the same
structural intuition we extend to the biomedical span level.
\citet{devaraj2021medical} identify safety-critical information loss as a
recurring failure in paragraph-level medical simplification, directly
motivating our preservation metrics. \citet{xia2025jebs} introduce JEBS
on the same five-operation PLABA taxonomy, arguing that separating
identification, classification, and generation enables more targeted
evaluation -- the diagnose-before-simplify principle underlying our
pipeline.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
SARI \citep{xu2016sari} rewards correct add/keep/delete operations against
multiple references; BERTScore \citep{zhang2020bertscore} adds contextual
semantic similarity. Neither captures whether a warning or dosage
survived, consistent with reports that SARI and FKGL correlate only
moderately with human judgment \citep{alvamanchego2021unsuitability} -- the
gap our preservation metrics are built to fill.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We show that diagnosing which operation a biomedical text span requires,
before generating output, is both learnable and useful. Our classifier
progression establishes that biomedical-domain pretraining, not general
contextual pretraining alone, drives operation-prediction accuracy, and a
corrected feature-integration ablation shows explicit UMLS jargon signal
provides real, coefficient-verified value beyond what a bag-of-words or
contextual encoder captures alone. Most centrally, our complete
operation-aware pipeline improves readability over safe-but-limited
rule-based substitution while matching its perfect safety profile on the
held-out test set, and narrows the gap to an unguided LLM's readability at
none of its safety cost -- and on the safety-critical, warning-stratified
subset of that same test set, our system is simultaneously safer \emph{and}
more readable than the unguided baseline, not merely safer at a cost. We
further show that complexity detectors are useful twice: once as
diagnostic input to a classifier, and again as an explicit, verified
guardrail during generation itself, a mechanism we believe generalizes
beyond this specific pipeline to any system combining a learned generator
with known safety-critical content spans.

Two threads remain open at the time of writing and are left for future
work. First, we have not yet tested whether concatenating our engineered
UMLS and numerical features onto BioBERT's contextual representation
improves classification specifically for Generalization, BioBERT's weakest
class, following the same ablation discipline as
Section~\ref{sec:feature-ablation} rather than assuming the TF-IDF result
transfers; UMLS features currently inform only the TF-IDF ablation and the
generation-time guardrail (Section~\ref{sec:chv-polish}), not the deployed
BioBERT classifier itself. Second, we plan a human evaluation (sample and
rubric already prepared, warning-stratified, three system outputs per
sentence) to corroborate our automatic preservation metrics against human
safety judgments directly.

\section*{Limitations}
Our jargon detection pipeline, while substantially more complete than a
morphological baseline, retains residual imprecision at the phrase level:
multi-word spans (e.g., \emph{urine output}) are not subject to our single-word
frequency filter and can be flagged despite being largely comprehensible to a lay
reader. We view this as an acceptable trade-off given diminishing returns on
further manual filtering, but note it explicitly rather than presenting our
jargon coverage figure as free of false positives. Our warning-preservation
metric's paraphrase map encodes annotator assumptions about semantic equivalence
and would benefit from a learned semantic-similarity replacement (e.g.,
BioBERT-based scoring) in future work.

Our operation labels are pseudo-labels derived from a length-ratio heuristic, not
gold annotations, and we have shown this heuristic's label distribution diverges
substantially from real human-annotated operation frequencies, particularly for
Generalization (Section~\ref{sec:classifier-results}); reported classifier
macro-F1 should be read as bounded jointly by model capacity and label quality,
not model capacity alone. Entity preservation currently relies on string matching
and does not credit valid paraphrases (e.g., \emph{hepatocellular carcinoma}
$\rightarrow$ \emph{liver cancer}) as preserved. Our warning-stratified
stress-test result on the test set (Table~\ref{tab:full-results-stratified}) is
based on $n=49$ sentences, small enough that individual sentences can shift the
reported preservation and SARI values by several points; we treat its magnitude
as indicative rather than a precise population estimate, though the qualitative
direction of the result replicates what was observed on the validation split
during development. The UMLS jargon matcher, while validated for corpus-level
coverage (Section~\ref{sec:complexity-results}) and used as a generation-time
guardrail input, has not yet been integrated as a feature into the deployed
BioBERT classifier itself (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses this as future
work). Finally, our pipeline is English-only and evaluated on a single dataset
(PLABA); generalization to other languages or biomedical sub-domains (e.g.,
mental health, pediatrics) is untested.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
This work addresses a genuine patient-safety concern: automated simplification
of biomedical text that silently drops warnings or dosages could cause direct
harm if deployed without safeguards. We want to be explicit that none of the
systems described here -- including our own operation-aware pipeline -- are
validated for unsupervised clinical deployment. Our preservation metrics improve
measurability of a known failure mode, but a metric passing a threshold is not
equivalent to clinical safety; any real-world use of automated biomedical
simplification should retain human oversight (e.g., pharmacist or clinician
review) before reaching patients, particularly for content involving dosing,
contraindications, or risk language. Our CHV-based Substitution pathway is
deliberately conservative (exact-match, curated dictionary) specifically to
minimize hallucination risk in the one component of our pipeline that could
otherwise introduce a factually incorrect substitution; we consider this a
necessary design choice for this domain rather than an accuracy-vs-safety
trade-off made lightly. We used only publicly available, de-identified research
abstracts (PLABA) with no patient-identifiable information, and all models used
(BioBERT, DistilBERT, FLAN-T5) are open-weight and locally run, so no data
processed by this pipeline is transmitted to a third-party API.
